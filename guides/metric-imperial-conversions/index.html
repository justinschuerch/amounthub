Metric and Imperial Conversion Guide for Project Estimates

Material calculations often combine metres, centimetres, feet, inches, square units, cubic units and weights. This guide explains the conversions that matter most when measuring projects and checking calculator results.

Length conversions

Length conversions are straightforward because they scale in one dimension. Problems usually appear when a length conversion is incorrectly reused for area or volume.

Area conversions are squared

A square metre is not simply 3.28 square feet. Because both length and width are converted, the factor is squared. One square metre equals about 10.7639 square feet, while one square foot equals about 0.0929 square metres.

Example: A floor measuring 4 m by 5 m covers 20 m². Converting the area directly gives about 215.28 ft². Converting each side first gives approximately 13.12 ft by 16.40 ft, which leads to the same area apart from rounding.

Volume conversions are cubed

Volume includes length, width and depth, so conversion factors are cubed. This is why a cubic metre is much larger than a cubic foot. One cubic metre is about 35.3147 cubic feet and about 1.30795 cubic yards.

For gravel, sand, soil, concrete and mulch, volume is usually the key intermediate quantity. Even if a supplier sells by weight, the project geometry starts with volume.

Depth conversions deserve extra attention

Small depth values create large errors when entered in the wrong unit. Five centimetres is 0.05 metres, not 0.5 metres. Likewise, 2 inches is about 0.167 feet, not 0.2 feet. Because depth is multiplied by the whole project area, a decimal mistake can multiply the final volume by ten or more.

A 20 m² area at 5 cm depth needs 1.0 m³ before allowances. If 5 cm is mistakenly entered as 0.5 m, the result becomes 10 m³ — a tenfold error.

Be careful with the word “ton.” A metric tonne is 1,000 kg, while a US short ton is 2,000 lb. They are not the same. AmountHub labels metric results as tonnes and imperial results as US tons to reduce ambiguity.

Density connects volume and weight

Bulk material weight is usually estimated by multiplying volume by density. If gravel has an assumed density of 1.70 tonnes per cubic metre, then 2 m³ of that gravel is estimated at 3.4 tonnes. The conversion is only as accurate as the density assumption, which is why supplier-specific density is better when available.

Moisture, grading and compaction can change density for gravel, sand and soil, so converted weight should be treated as a planning value rather than an exact load ticket.

Paint and coverage units

Paint introduces a different kind of conversion: coverage. Metric products often state square metres per litre, while US products may state square feet per gallon. One US gallon is about 3.78541 litres. However, converting the container volume alone is not enough; the product's stated coverage should be used because formulations differ.

Common unit mistakes

  1. Using metres for length but forgetting that a depth in centimetres must be converted before calculating volume.
  2. Converting m² to ft² using the linear 3.28 factor instead of the area factor 10.7639.
  3. Confusing cubic feet with cubic yards. One cubic yard contains 27 cubic feet.
  4. Confusing a metric tonne with a US short ton.
  5. Entering a product dimension in inches into a field intended for feet.
  6. Rounding too early. Keep a few decimal places during the calculation and round the final purchasing quantity.

Why AmountHub calculators switch systems internally

When a calculator offers Metric and Imperial modes, the safest implementation is to convert user inputs to a common internal unit, perform the calculation once, then display the result in the selected system. This avoids maintaining two unrelated formulas that can drift apart.

For example, a gravel calculation can convert feet and inches to metres, calculate area, volume and mass in metric internally, then convert the final result to cubic yards, US tons and pounds for display. This approach also makes it easier to test equivalent metric and imperial examples.

Quick sanity checks

Before trusting a converted result, make a rough mental check. A metre is a little longer than three feet. A square metre is a little under eleven square feet. A cubic metre is a little over one cubic yard. A kilogram is a little over two pounds. These approximations are not for final ordering, but they are excellent for spotting results that are obviously off by a factor of ten or more.
