Material Waste Allowances and Ordering Guide

The exact mathematical minimum is rarely the same as the quantity you should buy. Cuts, breakage, compaction, uneven surfaces and package sizes can all create a gap between a calculator result and a practical order.

What a waste allowance actually means

A waste allowance is extra material added to the theoretical quantity. It is not automatically “waste” that will be thrown away. Some of it may become offcuts, some may account for measurement uncertainty, and some may remain as useful spare material.

Different materials need different thinking. Gravel settles. Concrete can be lost in uneven excavation. Tile and flooring create cut pieces. Paint coverage can be lower on rough or absorbent surfaces. Bricks and pavers can break. That is why one universal percentage is not appropriate for every project.

Typical planning ranges

MaterialCommon planning allowanceWhy it may be needed
Gravel, sand, soilAbout 5–10%Settling, compaction, uneven ground and delivery losses.
ConcreteAbout 5–10%Uneven excavation, forms, spillage and small measurement errors.
TileAbout 10%; more for complex patternsCuts, breakage, pattern layout and future spare pieces.
FlooringAbout 5–10%End cuts, room shape and plank direction.
Pavers and bricksAbout 5–10%Edge cuts, breakage, pattern layout and damaged units.
DrywallAbout 10%Cutoffs, damaged sheets and layout constraints.

These are broad planning ranges, not rules. The correct allowance depends on the product, layout and installation method.

Why package size changes the final order

Even if a calculator says you need 21.3 m² of flooring, the product may be sold in packs covering 2.2 m² each. You cannot usually buy 0.68 of a pack, so the final order must be rounded up to a whole number of packs. The same applies to boxes of tile, bags of soil, rolls of wallpaper and containers of paint.

This is one reason a useful calculator should distinguish between theoretical coverage and purchasable units. AmountHub calculators for flooring, tile and bagged materials round the result in the direction that reflects how the product is normally bought.

When 10% is too much

Ten percent is convenient, but it can be unnecessarily expensive on large, simple projects. A 200 m² flooring job with a straightforward layout may not need the same percentage as a 10 m² bathroom with many corners. Large bulk orders can sometimes be planned with a smaller percentage because the absolute extra quantity is already substantial.

For high-cost materials, compare the cost of surplus against the risk and cost of a second delivery. One extra concrete truck or a second freight charge can cost more than a modest initial allowance.

When 10% may be too little

Complex patterns and irregular shapes can create more waste than a simple rectangle. Diagonal tile, herringbone flooring, curved paver edges and masonry with many openings may all require additional cut pieces. Products with natural variation may also need extra units so you can blend colours or reject damaged pieces.

If the same batch or colour lot may be difficult to replace later, keeping spare material can be especially valuable for tile, flooring and decorative finishes.

Bulk materials: think about density and compaction

Loose materials such as gravel, sand and soil are often sold by weight or volume. The conversion between the two depends on density, which changes with moisture, grading and compaction. If a supplier gives a product-specific density, use that value rather than a generic estimate.

Compaction also matters. A loose delivered pile may occupy more volume than the finished compacted layer. For a driveway or base course, the project specification may define compacted depth, while the supplier may discuss loose tonnage. Clarify which quantity is being quoted.

Concrete: running short is especially inconvenient

Concrete is difficult to “top up later” without consequences. If a pour is intended to be continuous, running short can create a construction joint in an unwanted location. Forms and excavation are also rarely perfect. This is why concrete orders often include a modest contingency after the theoretical slab volume is calculated.

However, too much surplus concrete also creates a disposal problem. For larger pours, confirm dimensions and ordering guidance with the ready-mix supplier.

Paint: coverage is not guaranteed

Paint manufacturers publish expected coverage rates, but actual usage depends on surface texture, porosity, colour change, application method and number of coats. Fresh drywall, rough masonry and strong colour transitions may consume more paint than a smooth previously painted wall.

Instead of adding a generic waste percentage, it is often more useful to use a realistic coverage rate and coat count in the Paint Calculator.

Keep useful leftovers

Some surplus is valuable. Spare tile can replace a cracked piece years later. Leftover flooring can help after water damage. A few matching bricks or pavers can be useful for repairs. Keep product labels and batch information with the spare material so future replacements are easier to identify.

By contrast, wet concrete and hot asphalt have limited reuse after delivery, so excess in those categories should be controlled more carefully.

A simple ordering checklist

  1. Measure the project carefully and write down the units.
  2. Calculate the theoretical quantity.
  3. Check whether the result is volume, weight, area or individual units.
  4. Apply a project-appropriate allowance instead of blindly using the same percentage every time.
  5. Round up to full bags, boxes, packs or rolls where necessary.
  6. Compare the result with supplier coverage, density or packaging information.
  7. For expensive or safety-critical work, confirm the order with the supplier or installer.
